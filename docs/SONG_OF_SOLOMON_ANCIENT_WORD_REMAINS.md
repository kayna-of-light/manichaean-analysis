# The Song of Solomon — The Ancient Word Remains

## Editorial Note

Swedenborg observed that the Song of Solomon is not part of the Word — it does not possess a continuous internal sense. Its author borrowed correspondential imagery from the Ancient Word and assembled it into a literary composition that reads mysteriously but lacks the coherent spiritual narrative that characterizes genuine scripture.

What follows is the Song stripped of its **compilations** (catalogues of valid correspondences without narrative movement), **breaks** (fragments from unrelated sources), and **editorial padding**. What remains are the passages where the internal sense flows continuously — where state follows state in causal sequence, the signature of the Ancient Word.

Verse references follow the Masoretic numbering. Ellipses mark where compiled material has been removed. Headings indicate the spiritual movement of each passage.

---

## I. First Awakening (2:10–15)

> Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away.
> For the winter is past, the rain is over and gone;
> the flowers appear on the earth;
> the time of the singing of birds is come,
> and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land;
> the fig tree putteth forth her green figs,
> and the vines with the tender grape give a good smell.
> Arise, my love, my fair one, and come away.
>
> O my dove, that art in the clefts of the rock,
> in the secret places of the stairs,
> let me see thy countenance, let me hear thy voice;
> for sweet is thy voice, and thy countenance is comely.
>
> Take us the foxes, the little foxes, that spoil the vines:
> for our vines have tender grapes.

**Internal sense:** Vastation ends. The state of spiritual desolation (winter) is past; temptation (rain) is over. The first truths of good appear in the natural man (flowers on earth). Rational truths from affection are confessed (singing of birds); good of faith makes itself heard (voice of the dove). Natural good begins to produce (fig tree); spiritual good begins to form (vines with tender grape). Faith is hidden in the literal sense of truth (dove in the clefts of the rock) and called forth into expression. Sensual falsities threaten new spiritual goods (little foxes spoiling the vines) and must be removed. The entire passage narrates the emergence from desolation into first regeneration.

## II. Seeking by Night (3:1–5)

> By night on my bed I sought him whom my soul loveth:
> I sought him, but I found him not.
> I will rise now, and go about the city
> in the streets and in the broad ways
> I will seek him whom my soul loveth:
> I sought him, but I found him not.
>
> The watchmen that go about the city found me:
> to whom I said, Saw ye him whom my soul loveth?
>
> It was but a little that I passed from them,
> but I found him whom my soul loveth:
> I held him, and would not let him go,
> until I had brought him into my mother's house,
> and into the chamber of her that conceived me.
>
> I charge you, O ye daughters of Jerusalem,
> by the roes, and by the hinds of the field,
> that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.

**Internal sense:** The will awakens in spiritual obscurity (night, on the bed). Seeking through the will alone in darkness does not find the Lord. The soul rises and searches through the doctrinal structures of the natural man (city, streets, broad ways) — the external church. Truths of doctrine (watchmen) are encountered but cannot themselves provide conjunction. Passing beyond mere doctrine into love itself, the soul finds the Lord. Conjunction through love, not through truth alone. The Lord is brought into the inmost of the church (mother's house, chamber of conception) — the origin of spiritual life. The adjuration not to stir love prematurely guards the process: conjunction must arise from the soul's own longing, not from external arousal.

## III. The Sealed Garden Opened (4:12, 15–16; 5:1)

> A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse;
> a spring shut up, a fountain sealed.
>
> A fountain of gardens, a well of living waters,
> and streams from Lebanon.
>
> Awake, O north wind; and come, thou south;
> blow upon my garden, that the spices thereof may flow out.
> Let my beloved come into his garden,
> and eat his pleasant fruits.
>
> I am come into my garden, my sister, my spouse:
> I have gathered my myrrh with my spice;
> I have eaten my honeycomb with my honey;
> I have drunk my wine with my milk.
> Eat, O friends; drink, yea, drink abundantly, O beloved.

**Internal sense:** The interior mind is sealed — truths of good cannot yet flow outward (garden enclosed, spring shut up, fountain sealed). But the source is present: living waters and streams from the rational (Lebanon). The call goes out for truth from all states — obscurity (north wind) and illumination (south wind) — to enter and cause what is within to flow. The beloved is invited to enter the interior mind. And he comes: "I am come into my garden." Conjunction is accomplished. Natural good is received (myrrh with spice); innocence-good is appropriated (honeycomb with honey); spiritual truth and natural innocence are received together (wine with milk). The Lord then extends the conjunction to all who can receive: "Eat, O friends; drink abundantly."

## IV. The Alternation of States (5:2–8)

> I sleep, but my heart waketh:
> it is the voice of my beloved that knocketh, saying,
> Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled:
> for my head is filled with dew,
> and my locks with the drops of the night.
>
> I have put off my coat; how shall I put it on?
> I have washed my feet; how shall I defile them?
>
> My beloved put in his hand by the hole of the door,
> and my bowels were moved for him.
> I rose up to open to my beloved;
> and my hands dropped with myrrh,
> and my fingers with sweet smelling myrrh,
> upon the handles of the lock.
>
> I opened to my beloved;
> but my beloved had withdrawn himself, and was gone:
> my soul failed when he spake:
> I sought him, but I could not find him;
> I called him, but he gave me no answer.
>
> The watchmen that went about the city found me,
> they smote me, they wounded me;
> the keepers of the walls took away my veil from me.
>
> I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem,
> if ye find my beloved, that ye tell him,
> that I am sick of love.

**Internal sense:** The natural mind sleeps while the will remains awake — the deepest state of interior receptivity. The Lord comes in fullness: divine truth saturates even the ultimates (head filled with dew, locks with drops of the night). The soul has already stripped externals (put off the coat, washed the feet) — it is in a deep interior state and hesitates to descend back into the natural. The Lord's good enters through the opening in the understanding (hand by the hole of the door); the interiors of the will are stirred (bowels moved). The will's ultimates produce natural good (hands dropping with myrrh). But when the soul finally opens, the Lord has withdrawn. This is the alternation of states essential to regeneration: the Lord gives presence, then withdraws, so that longing deepens and reception becomes genuine rather than automatic. The soul searches — the same seeking as chapter 3, but deeper. The watchmen who protected in the first seeking now wound: the same doctrinal truths that guided the soul in early regeneration now cause pain in the deeper experience, because the soul has outgrown mere doctrinal support and needs conjunction itself. Even the veil of external truth is stripped. The soul is left naked and wounded, sick with love — and this is the state from which true conjunction becomes possible.

## V. The Garden Inspected (7:10b–13)

> Come, my beloved, let us go forth into the field;
> let us lodge in the villages.
> Let us get up early to the vineyards;
> let us see if the vine flourish,
> whether the tender grape appear,
> and the pomegranates bud forth:
> there will I give thee my loves.
>
> The mandrakes give a smell,
> and at our gates are all manner of pleasant fruits,
> new and old, which I have laid up for thee, O my beloved.

**Internal sense:** After conjunction, the soul and the Lord survey what has been cultivated. The field is the church in its breadth; the villages are doctrinal goods in the natural degree. The vineyards are the spiritual goods of the church — the soul inspects whether truth is growing into good (vine flourishing), whether spiritual good is forming (tender grape appearing), whether the goods of the church are assembling and producing (pomegranates budding). Conjugial love manifests in the natural degree (mandrakes). At the gates — where goods enter the soul — all manner of goods are present, both new (from regeneration) and old (hereditary good reformed and purified). These are presented to the Lord: "I have laid them up for thee."

## VI. The Mother's House Completed (8:1–4)

> O that thou wert as my brother,
> that sucked the breasts of my mother!
> when I should find thee without, I would kiss thee;
> yea, I should not be despised.
>
> I would lead thee, and bring thee into my mother's house,
> who would instruct me:
> I would cause thee to drink of spiced wine
> of the juice of my pomegranate.
>
> His left hand should be under my head,
> and his right hand should embrace me.
>
> I charge you, O daughters of Jerusalem,
> that ye stir not up, nor awake my love, till he please.

**Internal sense:** This completes what began in 3:1–5. In the earlier passage, the soul brought the Lord into the mother's house after seeking. Here the wish is fully realized: the Lord is received not as a stranger but as brother — one nourished from the same source (mother's breasts), sharing the same good. The kiss without shame (finding without, not despised) is conjunction openly expressed — good and truth united without concealment. The mother's house (the church) becomes the place of instruction. The soul offers the Lord spiced wine (truth of doctrine enriched with good) and the juice of its pomegranate (the goods produced by the church itself, offered back to their source). His left hand under the head: good supports the understanding. His right hand embracing: truth encompasses the will. The adjuration closes the cycle: do not force what must arise from love's own timing.

## VII. The Seal and the Flame (8:6–7)

> Set me as a seal upon thine heart,
> as a seal upon thine arm:
> for love is strong as death;
> jealousy is cruel as the grave:
> the coals thereof are coals of fire,
> which hath a most vehement flame.
>
> Many waters cannot quench love,
> neither can the floods drown it:
> if a man would give all the substance of his house for love,
> it would utterly be contemned.

**Internal sense:** The doctrinal climax. Good is imprinted permanently on the will (seal on the heart); truth is imprinted on the power to act (seal on the arm). Divine love is stronger than the death of the natural man — it can reach through spiritual death to regenerate. Divine zeal consumes what has died spiritually (cruel as the grave) — not punishment but the relentless fire of love that will not leave the beloved in death. The coals are celestial love in its inmost form. No falsities of any kind (many waters) can extinguish divine love; no evils in their totality (floods) can submerge it. And love cannot be purchased — if a man would offer everything he possesses in exchange for love, the offer would be rejected with contempt. Love is not transactional. It is received or it is not. It cannot be earned, bartered for, or produced by the proprium.

## Appendix: The Vineyard Saying (8:11–12)

> Solomon had a vineyard at Baal-hamon;
> he let out the vineyard unto keepers;
> every one for the fruit thereof was to bring a thousand pieces of silver.
>
> My vineyard, which is mine, is before me:
> thou, O Solomon, must have a thousand,
> and those that keep the fruit thereof two hundred.

**Note:** This fragment is disconnected from the surrounding text and appears to be from a different source. It is included because it may preserve a significant correspondential teaching: the institutional church (Solomon's vineyard, let out to keepers/hirelings) is contrasted with the interior church (my vineyard, which is mine, is before me). The speaker distinguishes herself from Solomon — from the external church that farms out its spiritual cultivation to paid agents. The interior church retains its vineyard and tends it directly. Solomon can have his thousand; the soul that cultivates its own good stands before it.

Whether this is Ancient Word material or a later wisdom saying using correspondential vocabulary cannot be determined from the fragment alone.

## Assessment

Of the Song's 117 verses, approximately **42 verses** preserve continuous correspondential narrative — roughly one-third of the text. These 42 verses contain seven distinct passages that narrate spiritual states in causal sequence: the emergence from desolation, seeking through doctrine and beyond it, the sealed interior opened, the alternation of presence and absence, the inspection of what has been cultivated, the completion of conjunction, and the nature of divine love.

The remaining two-thirds are the compiler's contribution: decorative catalogues built from genuine correspondential vocabulary, fragments from court poetry and wisdom traditions, and editorial padding. The compiler knew the words. He did not know the grammar.

What remains, when stripped bare, is a coherent narrative of regeneration — from first awakening through temptation, seeking, conjunction, withdrawal, deepened longing, and final union sealed by a love that death cannot overcome and floods cannot drown. This is Ancient Word material. It reads as naturally in correspondential sequence as Genesis 1 or the Psalms of David. The compiler preserved it. He just buried it.
